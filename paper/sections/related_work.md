# 2. Related Work

Our work draws from and bridges four distinct research areas: PCA-based process monitoring, streaming PCA algorithms, online feature selection, and edge AI for sensor networks. We organize the literature along two axes—*static vs.\ streaming* and *fixed-rule vs.\ data-driven*—to identify the gap our method fills.

## 2.1 PCA for Process Monitoring and Fault Detection

Principal Component Analysis has been the workhorse of industrial process monitoring for over two decades. The standard approach projects high-dimensional sensor data onto a reduced subspace and monitors Hotelling's T² and Squared Prediction Error (SPE) statistics to detect deviations from normal operation [8, 9]. On the Tennessee Eastman Process benchmark, PCA-based methods achieve fault detection rates exceeding 93% when combined with preprocessing techniques such as lag augmentation and moving-average filtering [8]. Dynamic PCA (DPCA) extends the framework to capture temporal autocorrelations by augmenting observations with time-lagged copies, while Kernel PCA (KPCA) handles nonlinear relationships through implicit feature-space mappings [9].

Recent surveys confirm that PCA and its variants remain competitive with deep learning approaches for many fault types, with the added advantages of interpretability and low computational cost [9]. LSTM-based classifiers achieve 98.86% accuracy on TEP, but at substantially higher computational cost. Crucially, the loading vectors from PCA decomposition reveal which sensors contribute most to each principal component—a property widely used for *post-hoc fault diagnosis* to identify root causes after a fault is detected [8, 17]. SPE contribution analysis, for instance, highlights that variables such as reactor temperature (XMEAS 9) and valve positions (XMV 3, XMV 4) dominate during specific fault types, while remaining quiescent during others [8]—precisely the kind of dynamic importance shift our method aims to detect and exploit in real time.

Mnassri et al. [17] benchmark multiple feature selection approaches on TEP, including mutual information (max-dependency, max-relevance, min-redundancy) and PCA-based selection, confirming that different faults activate different sensor subsets. This finding is central to our motivation: a static, uniform sampling strategy wastes bandwidth on sensors that are uninformative for the current operating regime.

However, all existing PCA-based monitoring methods share a fundamental assumption: *full-resolution data from every sensor is available at every time step*. None address the practical scenario—common in bandwidth-constrained IoT deployments—where the monitoring system must decide *which channels to sample at higher rates* before data collection occurs. Our method repurposes PCA's loading-based importance analysis from post-hoc diagnosis to *proactive data acquisition*, converting weighted loading scores into per-channel sampling rates under a total bandwidth budget.

## 2.2 Streaming PCA and Online Feature Selection

**Streaming PCA.** A rich literature exists on computing principal components from sequential data. Oja's rule [25] provides the foundational online update for rank-1 PCA using Hebbian learning at O(d) per sample. CCIPCA [5] extends this to multiple components without covariance estimation at O(dk) cost, using an amnesic average for non-stationary data. GROUSE [6] formulates subspace tracking as gradient descent on the Grassmannian manifold, naturally handling missing observations with proven linear convergence. History PCA [7] achieves faster convergence by retaining a small block of recent samples in O(Bd) memory. PAST [27] and PETRELS [28] offer second-order and parallelizable variants, respectively. Balzano, Chi, and Lu [26] provide a comprehensive survey comparing these algorithms across missing-data scenarios and time-varying subspaces.

Recent work on low-precision streaming PCA [34] demonstrates that subspace estimation remains an active area, with new algorithms achieving convergence guarantees under quantization constraints relevant to edge deployment. These algorithms efficiently produce updated subspace estimates from streaming data. However, their output—an orthonormal basis matrix U—is not directly actionable for sensor management. None include a mechanism to convert subspace information into per-channel sampling decisions under a bandwidth budget.

**Online Feature Selection.** A parallel body of work addresses streaming feature selection, where features arrive sequentially and must be accepted or rejected in real time. Alpha-investing [12] controls false discovery rates through dynamic significance thresholds but ignores redundancy. OSFS [13] adds redundancy removal via conditional independence tests, finding an approximate Markov blanket. SAOLA [11] achieves scalability through pairwise comparisons with theoretical information-gain bounds. OSFSW [3] and its self-adaptive variant [16] use sliding windows to handle high-redundancy streams. A comprehensive 2022 survey [4] catalogs these methods across data forms including group streams, multi-label, and concept drift scenarios, and identifies feature interaction and imbalanced streaming as open challenges.

Most recently, OSSFS [35] (2025) introduces feature aggregation via cohesive MeanShift to improve selection *stability*—a previously overlooked criterion—but remains binary and supervised. Zhou et al. [36] (2024) extend DSRA with parametric machine learning to auto-tune temporal sampling parameters, but still apply a single rate to all channels.

All streaming feature selection methods share two limitations relative to our setting. First, they produce *binary* decisions—a feature is either selected or discarded—rather than the *proportional* rate allocation our method provides. Second, they are inherently *supervised*, requiring labeled data for relevance testing, whereas our PCA-based approach is entirely unsupervised. Feature selection for industrial process data has been studied in the batch setting [17, 18], confirming that different faults activate different sensor subsets—precisely the dynamic our adaptive method exploits.

## 2.3 Adaptive Sampling in IoT and Sensor Networks

Adaptive sampling in IoT aims to reduce energy and bandwidth consumption by adjusting data acquisition strategies. Ben-Aboud et al. [1] propose lightweight algorithms using Kalman predictions to adapt sampling intervals, tested on air pollution and temperature data, achieving noticeable computational load reduction over state-of-the-art methods. Giordano et al. [2] develop an energy-harvesting-aware approach using AIMD-style rate control inspired by TCP Reno, achieving 85–95% of theoretical optimal throughput with only 527 CPU cycles per day and O(1) memory—a remarkable demonstration of what lightweight adaptive algorithms can achieve on microcontrollers. The Dynamic Sampling Rate Algorithm (DSRA) adjusts sampling frequency based on signal variance, saving up to 30% battery energy in water-quality monitoring. Send-on-Delta (SoD) methods transmit only when sensor readings change by a threshold, naturally reducing bandwidth for stable signals. Data reduction of up to 79% has been reported without undermining measurement quality [1].

These methods effectively answer *"how often should I sample?"* but treat all sensors identically—the same temporal rate applies to every channel. None ask *"which channels deserve more of my limited bandwidth?"*

Compressive sensing (CS) [30] offers an alternative data reduction paradigm, exploiting signal sparsity to reconstruct from sub-Nyquist samples via random projections and $\ell_1$-minimization. While CS achieves impressive compression ratios, it requires (a) that the signal be sparse in some known basis, (b) random measurement matrices that may be impractical to implement in hardware, and (c) computationally expensive reconstruction at the receiver. Our approach makes no sparsity assumption, uses deterministic rate allocation based on PCA importance, and incurs no reconstruction cost—the receiver simply processes whatever data arrives at the allocated rates.

Our PCA-triage method answers *"which channels deserve more of my limited bandwidth?"* Importantly, it is *orthogonal to and combinable with* temporal adaptation: one could first apply PCA-triage to allocate a per-channel bandwidth budget, then use DSRA or SoD within each channel to further optimize temporal sampling.

## 2.4 Edge AI and Sensor Data Processing

Edge AI research focuses on deploying machine learning on resource-constrained devices through model compression, knowledge distillation, split computing, and early-exit strategies [10]. Gill et al. [10] present the first comprehensive taxonomy of Edge AI spanning 11 dimensions—from infrastructure to container scaling—and identify that deploying AI on resource-constrained edge devices "requires approaches adapted to processing limitations." The survey distinguishes static IoT (environmental monitoring, surveillance) from mobile IoT (wearables, vehicles), both of which generate high-volume sensor streams that strain network bandwidth. Notably, the entire Edge AI literature focuses on *model-side* optimization: making the inference model smaller or faster. The complementary problem of *data-side* optimization—reducing what sensor data is collected and transmitted—receives comparatively little attention.

Attention mechanisms offer a data-driven alternative for assessing sensor importance but carry significant computational overhead. Multi-attention feature selection (m-AFS) [19] uses parallel temporal and variate attention modules, correctly identifying relevant variables and time lags on synthetic data, but requires 10–173 seconds per evaluation depending on dataset size. Transformer-based anomaly detectors [20] model inter-variable correlations through O(d²) self-attention, providing rich importance scores at the cost of quadratic scaling in the number of sensors. Dual-channel feature fusion models [21] achieve strong F1 scores (0.89–0.94) on NASA SMAP and SMD benchmarks but at 51ms per decision with 325K parameters—3–28× slower than simpler methods and far too heavy for microcontroller deployment. Graph attention networks [22, 23] capture sensor interdependencies through learned adjacency structures but require graph construction and multi-head attention computation, adding both memory and latency overhead.

The entire Edge AI literature optimizes the *model side* of the inference pipeline—compressing what processes the data. Our method optimizes the *data side*—compressing what data reaches the model. PCA-triage runs at O(wdk) per window with zero trainable parameters, achieving channel importance scoring at a fraction of the cost of attention-based alternatives. The two approaches are complementary: PCA-triage reduces input dimensionality, while model compression reduces inference cost.

## 2.5 Positioning and Contribution

Table 1 positions our contribution along the two key axes of the literature.

| | **Static / Batch** | **Streaming / Adaptive** |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed Rules** | Uniform sampling; Threshold-based (SoD) | DSRA [1]; Energy-aware AIMD [2] |
| **Data-Driven** | Batch PCA detection [8,9]; Offline FS [17,18] | **PCA-Triage (Ours)** |

No existing method occupies the bottom-right cell: a *streaming, data-driven* approach that converts PCA loadings into *proportional per-channel bandwidth allocation* under a total budget constraint. Adaptive sampling methods adapt temporally but not spatially; PCA methods diagnose but do not triage; feature selection methods select but do not allocate proportionally; attention mechanisms allocate but are too expensive for edge deployment. Our method bridges these gaps with an unsupervised, lightweight algorithm that runs in <5ms per decision on commodity hardware.
